Couples Bucket List: 100 Ideas You’ll Actually Do Together

Updated October 2026 · 10 min read

A couples bucket list is easy to write and hard to live. Most lists collect twenty good ideas in a note, and then nothing on them happens until someone finds the note two years later.

The ideas below are grouped by kind, from small outdoor plans to once-in-a-lifetime trips. Read through them together, mark the ones you both react to, then use the short method at the end to actually get one on the calendar.

Outdoors

  1. Watch a sunrise from somewhere high. A hill, a rooftop, a lookout. Bring something warm to drink and leave before it gets crowded.
  2. Sleep under the stars once. A campsite, a backyard, or a dark-sky park. One night counts.
  3. Hike to a waterfall. Pick one within driving distance and go in the season it’s fullest.
  4. Paddle somewhere calm at dawn. Rent a canoe or kayak before the wind picks up.
  5. See a meteor shower properly. Check the date of the next big one, drive away from city light, and lie on a blanket.
  6. Walk an entire trail, start to finish. A local long trail in sections counts. Mark each section off together.
  7. Pick your own fruit. Strawberries in early summer, apples in fall. Then make something with them.
  8. Find a hot spring. Natural or developed, the point is the cold air and the warm water.
  9. Go snowshoeing. Easier than skiing, quieter than almost anything else in winter.
  10. Plant something you’ll both watch grow. A tree, a herb box, a balcony garden. Something you’ll check on together.

Travel

  1. Take a trip with no fixed plan. Book the first night only and decide the rest as you go.
  2. Ride an overnight train. Sleeping while the landscape changes outside is its own kind of trip.
  3. Visit the place one of you grew up. Let them give the tour: old school, old hangouts, the corner shop.
  4. Spend a week on a small island. Somewhere with one road, one shop and nothing urgent to do.
  5. Take a road trip along a coast. Stop wherever looks good. Keep a list of the best places you didn’t expect.
  6. Go somewhere neither of you speaks the language. Learn ten phrases together before you leave.
  7. See the northern lights. Plan it for the right season and give yourselves several nights for the sky to clear.
  8. Stay somewhere unusual. A treehouse, a lighthouse, a houseboat, a cabin with no road access.
  9. Do a trip by bike. A few days between towns, luggage sent ahead if you want it easy.
  10. Return to the place you had your first trip together. Notice what’s changed, there and between you.

Food and drink

  1. Take a cooking class in the cuisine you order most. Then try making it at home the following week.
  2. Do a tasting menu once. Pick a special occasion and save for it.
  3. Learn to make fresh pasta together. Flour, eggs, one evening, a lot of mess.
  4. Visit a vineyard, brewery or cider farm. Book the tour, not just the tasting.
  5. Eat your way through one street. Pick a market or food street and share one small dish at every stop.
  6. Cook a dish from each other’s family. Ask a parent or grandparent for the real recipe.
  7. Bake bread from scratch. Sourdough if you’re patient, focaccia if you’re not.
  8. Take a sommelier or coffee-tasting course. Learning to taste the same thing differently is surprisingly fun.
  9. Forage with a guide. Mushrooms, wild garlic, berries—always with someone who knows.
  10. Host a dinner party you plan together. Split the menu, set the table properly, invite people you both like.

Culture

  1. See a show in a city known for them. Broadway, the West End, or the best theater near you.
  2. Go to an opera or ballet once. Neither of you has to love it. Dress up anyway.
  3. Attend a music festival. Camp if you’re brave; book a hotel if you’ve done that before.
  4. Read the same book and talk about it. A two-person book club with one rule: finish it.
  5. Visit a museum you’ve both never heard of. Small, odd museums often make the best stories.
  6. Watch a film in an outdoor cinema. Bring the blanket and the snacks you actually like.
  7. See your favorite band live. Or the band one of you loved at sixteen.
  8. Take a photography walk. Each shoot the same street for an hour and compare.
  9. Watch every film by one director. Take turns choosing the director.
  10. Go to a cultural festival you’ve never experienced. A lantern festival, a harvest fair, a celebration from another tradition.

Sports and adventure

  1. Get a diving certification together. A course gives you a new skill and a reason to travel.
  2. Go skydiving or paragliding. Tandem counts. Decide in advance who goes first.
  3. Learn to surf. Book lessons for at least three days so it starts to click.
  4. Run a race together. A 5K, a fun run, or something bigger if you both want it.
  5. Try indoor climbing. Belay each other and learn to trust a rope.
  6. Charter a boat for a weekend. With a skipper if neither of you sails.
  7. Go white-water rafting. Pick a grade that’s exciting but not reckless.
  8. Ski or snowboard for the first time. Learning badly together beats watching each other be good.
  9. Take a hot-air balloon ride. Early start, quiet sky, one very good photo.
  10. Do a via ferrata. Fixed cables let beginners reach real mountain views.

Wellness

  1. Spend a day at a spa. Phones off from arrival to leaving.
  2. Take a weekend with no screens. Tell people in advance. Notice how long the first afternoon feels.
  3. Try a sauna and cold plunge. Hot, cold, rest, repeat—and talk in between.
  4. Do a yoga retreat. A weekend is enough to know whether you like it.
  5. Take a couples massage class. Learn something you can actually use at home.
  6. Walk somewhere every morning for a month. Same time, same route, no agenda.
  7. Do a short silent retreat. Then compare notes afterwards.
  8. Cook every meal at home for two weeks. Plan the menu together on Sunday.
  9. Stay in a cabin with no Wi-Fi. Bring books, a deck of cards and fewer plans than usual.
  10. Float in a sensory deprivation tank. Separate tanks, one strange story to share after.

Romance

  1. Recreate your first date. Same place, same order, same questions if you remember them.
  2. Write each other letters to open in five years. Seal them, date them, and put the date in your calendar.
  3. Take a dance class and go out dancing. Salsa, swing, tango—then use it somewhere real.
  4. Renew or write your own vows. Read them aloud, even if it’s only to each other.
  5. Plan a surprise trip for the other person. They get the dates and the packing list. Nothing else.
  6. Have a portrait taken together. A real photographer, not a phone at arm’s length.
  7. Watch the sunset from a boat. A ferry counts if that’s what’s nearby.
  8. Make a playlist of your relationship. One song for every year or every big moment.
  9. Stay a night in a hotel in your own city. Room service, late checkout, no chores at home.
  10. Celebrate an anniversary somewhere new every year. Keep a list of where you’ve been.

Learning

  1. Learn a language together. Pick one tied to a place you’d like to go.
  2. Take a pottery course. Keep the lopsided first bowl.
  3. Learn an instrument at the same time. Not the same instrument. Then try a song together.
  4. Do a first-aid course. Less romantic, genuinely useful.
  5. Learn to sail. A beginner course over a couple of weekends.
  6. Take a woodworking or furniture class. Build one thing you’ll keep in your home.
  7. Learn a dish from a country you’ve visited. Recreate the meal you still talk about.
  8. Take an improv class. You will both be bad at it. That’s the point.
  9. Learn the stars. Download a star map and learn ten constellations from your window.
  10. Teach each other something. Each pick a skill you have and the other doesn’t.

With friends

  1. Rent a big house with friends for a weekend. Assign meals, not activities.
  2. Host a themed dinner. Choose a decade, a country, or a film.
  3. Do an escape room as a team. See who takes charge. It’s often not who you’d think.
  4. Organize a group camping trip. One shared fire, everyone’s food.
  5. Throw a party you plan together. A birthday, a housewarming, or no reason at all.
  6. Do a group volunteer day. A beach clean, a food bank shift, a trail repair.
  7. Start a regular game night. Same night every month so it actually happens.
  8. Travel with another couple. Agree in advance on time apart as well as together.
  9. Run a trivia team. A terrible team name is mandatory.
  10. Go to a wedding as a team. Plan the outfits, learn one dance, stay until the end.

Seasonal

  1. Go to a Christmas market abroad. Mulled wine, cold hands, one ornament you buy together.
  2. Watch the cherry blossoms. Their timing shifts year to year, so plan loosely.
  3. Spend New Year’s Eve somewhere new. Or skip the crowds and watch fireworks from a hill.
  4. Do a summer bucket list. Ten things for one summer, posted somewhere you’ll see.
  5. Pick a pumpkin and carve it. Roast the seeds.
  6. Swim in the sea on a cold day. Short, loud, and memorable.
  7. See fall colors on a drive. Pick a scenic route at peak week.
  8. Have a picnic on the first warm day of the year. Wherever you can get to in twenty minutes.
  9. Build a snowman or go sledding. Act younger than you are.
  10. Celebrate a festival from the other person’s culture. Let them lead and learn the traditions properly.

How to turn the list into plans

Sort what you picked into three groups:

  • Someday: ideas you both like but haven’t scheduled. This is most of the list, and that’s fine.
  • Planned: anything with a real date. Aim to always have at least one.
  • Dreams: the big, once-in-a-lifetime ones with no deadline. Keep them separate so they don’t make the rest feel small.

Then do one thing: take a single Someday idea and give it a date. Not “this spring,” an actual day. Research on planning has found that deciding when and where you’ll do something makes you meaningfully more likely to follow through than simply intending to, with a medium-to-large effect across 94 studies (Gollwitzer and Sheeran, 2006). Couples studies also suggest that doing new and exciting things together is linked to feeling better about the relationship afterwards (Aron et al., 2000), so favor the ideas neither of you has done.

When it’s done, keep something: a photo, the ticket, one sentence about how it felt. A list of things you’ve actually lived is worth more than a longer list of things you might.

Frequently asked questions

What should be on a couples bucket list?

A mix of small plans you could do this month, a few bigger trips or experiences you’d save for, and one or two once-in-a-lifetime dreams. Ideas neither of you has done before are a good place to start: research links new shared experiences with feeling better about the relationship.

How do you make a couples bucket list?

Each of you writes down ideas separately, then you compare and keep the ones you both like. Sort them into someday ideas, planned ideas with a date, and big dreams, and schedule at least one.

How many things should be on a couples bucket list?

There’s no right number. Anywhere from ten to fifty ideas gives you plenty to choose from without the list becoming a chore.
